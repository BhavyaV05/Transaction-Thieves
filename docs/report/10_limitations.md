The claims of this report are about a synthetic benchmark with simulated banks. This section lists what could make a number in section 7 wrong or too good for reasons other than the method (threats to validity) and what the study does not cover (limitations). Each item says how we guard against it, and where the guard is weak.

## 10.1 Threats to validity

**Leakage and look-ahead.** A system that sees the future or the labels would look good for the wrong reason. The guards are in code and each has a known weakness.

- *The test lock.* The test split loads only when `FTM_FINAL_RUN=1`, set at G5 only (`ftm/guards.py`); the final-run script builds the external subset first and records its hash before any system runs (section 6.2). This protects against us, not against a mistake inside the final run, so a rerun after seeing test results would break it; there is one final run.
- *The time guard.* Every tool query takes an `as_of` time and sees only data at or before it, and accounts are given random aliases in everything an agent reads, so an LLM cannot recognise a famous account. An alias hides the identity, not the behaviour; a long enough history can identify an account's pattern.
- *Train-only memory.* The case memory of Sharma, Torres and the other Group C ports, the strategy library and the FinFRE-RAG exemplar pool are built from train alerts only, and the pool refuses any other split. The library is filed by the *shape* of the situation, not by account, because AMLworld repeats laundering rings and a library keyed by account would memorise known criminals; the by-account ablation and the shuffled-answers test check that no gain comes from memorisation.
- *Look-ahead inside the detection models.* The graph models score a validation edge on the graph of train plus the whole validation split, so a validation edge can have later validation edges among its neighbours (on HI-Small, 12.6% of an edge's incident edges are later on average; `ftm/baselines/README.md`). This is the protocol of Multi-GNN and of the graph-feature model's own batches, and it affects the validation numbers of the one-bank and see-everything models used for the denominators of gap closed, so it is documented and not removed. How far it carries over to test is {{TBD:tab_baselines}}.
- *Selecting on the labels that are then used.* The back-test accepts a question using train labels, and the accepted likelihood ratios are then used as if they were unbiased. On validation this is the most likely cause of the naive-fusion failure (section 5.4.4): a ratio of 0.49 on train against 1.15 on validation for one accepted question. Error control by e-values protects the accepted *set*, not the size or even the sign of each estimate, and the validation data are later in time. Fitting the fusion on validation (cross-fitted) corrects the weights but spends validation data that is already small.

**Back-test caches.** Back-test results are cached on disk under a namespace that hashes the alert set, seed and configuration, so a stale entry is never read (`ftm/backtest/README.md`). The cost is that two runs that look the same are not the same cache: the validation numbers of section 5.4.4 come from the namespaces `4338ebb59b35` (HI-Small, 1 accepted question of 440) and `7df6bad5337d` (HI-Medium, 23 accepted), and any change of alert set, seed or configuration starts a new back-test with new accepted questions. A figure or table is only comparable with another if both name the same namespace; each table of section 7 lists the namespace it was built from.

**Clusters and intervals.** Alerts of one laundering attempt share accounts, so intervals must resample whole clusters. On alerts the largest cluster is small (3.0% of HI-Small validation alerts, 1.5% of HI-Medium), but over all rows the giant component grows (16.3% on HI-Small validation and 58% on HI-Medium rows, `docs/t4_decisions_week2.md`, `STATUS.md`) because hub accounts chain unrelated rows together. The hub cap that breaks those chains is a choice made on validation ({{TBD:hub_cap}}), and the end-to-end numbers over all rows depend on it more than the numbers on alerts do. Cluster bootstrap also assumes that clusters are independent, and the data come from a single simulation run per dataset, so correlation across clusters that the generator induces is not captured.

**Small samples.** Askable alerts are the alerts whose partner bank is one of the 30 modelled bank views, and only they can be questioned. In the validation `eval` part there are 87 askable alerts on HI-Small (17 positive) and 160 on HI-Medium (52 positive); across all HI-Medium validation parts there are 460 (208 positive) (`docs/t1_handoffs.md`, A4). On the train sets, 1,983 of 10,962 HI-Medium alerts (and 1,038 of 5,198 on HI-Small) are askable. The share of askable alerts on the test set is {{TBD:n_askable_test}}. The intervals on these samples are wide (the Brier gain of fitted fusion on HI-Medium is +0.0053 with an interval from -0.041 to +0.054), and the study can only separate large differences, which is why differences below the margin are never reported as "equivalent" unless the interval says so (section 6.4). Validation is also used for several things at once (conformal calibration, fusion weights, the backbone sweep, the G2 check), which is why fusion is cross-fitted inside it and why we do not choose settings by looking at variants and keeping the best.

**Intake auto-clears most laundering.** At the alert budget the conformal intake clears about 60% of laundering transactions without any agent seeing them (`docs/t4_decisions_week2.md`, item 2). Gap closed on alerts is therefore not what a bank recovers end to end, and every result is shown at both levels with the intake miss rate next to it (section 6.3). The final intake miss rate and alert counts are {{TBD:n_alerts}}.

**One dataset family.** Both datasets, HI-Small and HI-Medium, come from the same generator, AMLworld [1], whose laundering patterns (fan-in, fan-out, cycles, scatter-gather and so on) were written by the authors; a method that learns to recognise these templates may not transfer to real laundering, which is messier and rarer. The held-out-cycles experiment (E12) tests transfer to a pattern removed from training and is a partial answer only. We have no real data and make no claim about real banks.

**A single backbone and conditional conclusions.** Every LLM system runs on the backbone chosen by the size sweep (a small open model, 8B by default, `configs/llm/default.yaml`) under a cap of 20 calls and 6,000 prompt tokens per alert. A finding such as "the external agent does worse" is a finding about that backbone and that budget; with a larger model or more calls it could change. A frontier-model run on a few hundred alerts is a stretch item and is only run if time allows.

## 10.2 Limitations of the design

**Synthetic banks and simulated private set intersection.** Partner banks are nodes that answer from their own ledger in the simulator; no institution is involved, replies are instantaneous apart from a simulated latency, and the private set intersection of [36] is simulated by its noise, with no cryptography. What the questions would recover from real, messier ledgers, with different field definitions and data quality, is not measured.

**The privacy guarantee is per transaction.** Differential privacy [32] here protects the addition or removal of one transaction, which is weaker than protecting a customer, and questions that return customer-level records are listed as restricted and are outside the guarantee. The per-account cap on the total ε spent on one partner account (provisionally 8, `prereg/hypotheses.yaml`) and the separate calibration budget are our response, with basic composition, which is loose compared with tighter accountants. A cap of 8 refused 1 to 3% of the questions of a simulated stream (`docs/t1_handoffs.md`, A3), but the stream is two questions per alert, not what a planner asks, so the true effect of the cap is measured only in the final run.

**Approximate guarantees under drift.** The conformal intake and the false-discovery-rate control of the back-test hold only under exchangeability or related assumptions, which day-to-day drift breaks, and the same is true of the three-way decision (FCAC [40]) if it is built, which is controlled only under its own audit assumption. We therefore report the rates we actually get next to the targets and do not claim the guarantee holds.

**Reimplemented external systems.** Most external agents are reimplementations from papers that give no prompts or settings (section 6.2). Their published numbers are on other datasets and cannot be checked on AMLworld, so a reimplementation may be weaker than the original; we fix every open choice before the first run, label each row, and do not tune after seeing results, but the doubt remains and applies to the comparison of section 7.4.

**The planner's questions and what it learns.** The planner can only ask questions that the question language can express and that the accepted list contains. On validation the accepted list is short (1 of 440 questions on HI-Small and 23 of 440 on HI-Medium pass realistic back-testing), and the planner asks no accepted question there, so its Brier equals p0 (`docs/schedule_nov10.md`, rule 6). Whether the questions recover a meaningful share of the gap on test is the result of the final run and is stated in section 7 whichever way it falls; the G2 decision is {{TBD:g2_decision}}.

**Dual use.** A system that learns which questions clear an account could help criminals. We release the code, the simulator, the frozen alert sets and the harness, and we do not release trained weights (section 8).

**Items cut or not run.** The cut order is in `plan.md` section 14 (the RL planner, the frontier-API run, stretch external agents, learning from test labels, the one-at-a-time study, SemIf and Laya, the conformal cascade and FCAC, in that order, with the core never cut). What was actually cut, and what each cut changes in the claims, is {{TBD:cut_items}}.

## 10.3 Future work

Four directions follow from what is already known. First, a one-bank model that leaves the questioned patterns out of p0 would test the double-counting explanation directly, which fusion alone cannot do (`docs/t4_fusion_findings.md`, section 5). Second, re-estimating or shrinking the likelihood ratios on a time-adjacent part of train, before fusion, would address the shift explanation without touching validation. Third, a protection unit larger than a transaction, with a tighter accountant, would make the privacy claim stronger and would change the privacy-detection curve. Fourth, testing the questions on a second generator or on real data under a data-sharing agreement would show whether the result is specific to AMLworld's templates; this is the limitation that matters most for any practical claim.

## 2.1 AML data and detection

**Data.** AMLworld [1] is the synthetic multi-bank transaction data from IBM that we use (Kaggle, CDLA-Sharing licence). Because it comes from a simulator, every transaction has a correct laundering label and we know which laundering pattern it belongs to, among a fixed set of pattern types (fan-in, fan-out, cycle, scatter-gather and others). The authors used it to show that a model looking at one bank's slice does much worse than a model that sees everything [1]. Their detection model is a gradient-boosted tree classifier on graph features computed by the Graph Feature Preprocessor (GFP) of Blanuša et al. [2], which is the "one bank only" and "see everything" model of our baselines. The published see-everything figure on HI-Small is 63.23 F1 [1], [2].

**Stronger detection models.** Several later methods report higher scores on the same data and are used in this work both as an alternative starting model for each bank and as a stronger "see everything" upper limit (plan section 6.2). Multi-GNN [42] is an edge-classification graph neural network for directed multigraphs with official code under Apache-2.0 that runs on HI-Small. FraudGT [43] is a graph transformer for financial fraud detection; its repository declares no licence, so its code cannot be copied into ours without asking the authors. MEGA-GNN [44] aggregates multi-edges in a multigraph, has Apache-2.0 code, and in its own paper reports a HI-Small minority-class F1 of 74.88 (MEGA-GenAgg) and 74.69 (MEGA(PNA)-GIN) against 68.16 for the Multi-PNA baseline (`docs/t1_week0.md`). ExSTraQt [75] is a supervised, non-GNN method on engineered quasi-temporal graph features; it is the source of the roughly 80 F1 figure that benchmark aggregators list for HI-Small [74]: 78.90 with the GFP-style split and 80.89 with the FraudGT split, mean of 5 seeds (`docs/t1_week0.md`). [CHECK: the same document could not confirm whether ExSTraQt's decision threshold was tuned on test or what the FraudGT split ratios are; verify both in the paper before quoting these numbers]

These numbers are not comparable across papers. They are computed on different splits (the aggregator lists FraudGT at 76.41, Multi-GNN at 68.16 and GFP at 64.77 next to ExSTraQt, `docs/t1_week0.md`), whereas this work trains every model on its own temporal split of days 1-9, 10-12 and 13-16 and re-runs each model on it. We reproduce a baseline on its paper's split first, as a check on our implementation, and report the numbers on our split separately.

**What is left open.** All of these models either see one bank's view or see the whole graph. None asks what a bank could recover by querying other banks for specific accounts while keeping their data in place. This work uses them as the two ends of the scale (gap closed is computed against both the GFP upper limit and the best state-of-the-art upper limit) and asks how much of the gap private questions close (RQ1, RQ6).

## 2.2 Federated and privacy-preserving cross-bank work

**Federated AML.** Project Aurora [7], by the Bank for International Settlements, studies combating money laundering across institutions and borders with data, technology and collaboration. [CHECK: describe what Aurora actually evaluated, from the report itself; the repo only characterises it, in Table 1 of the proposal, among the "federated AML" work in which banks train jointly or compute on pooled data] Tian et al. [8] (WWW 2025) propose collaborative AML among financial institutions; their public code reads AMLSim and AMLworld HI-Small with its patterns file and treats institutions as clients, which matches our bank split, but the repository declares no licence (`docs/t1_week0.md`). We chose it as the AML-specific federated comparison for that reason, with Ceydeli et al. [9] reimplemented as the fallback. Ceydeli et al. [9] address cross-client subgraph pattern detection; the arXiv page is now titled "Federated Layer-Wise Exchange for Cross-Client Money Laundering Pattern Detection", no code was found, and the idea is a layer-wise exchange between clients that, per `docs/t1_week0.md`, matches centralised training. Two further candidates were ruled out for lack of code or of AMLworld results: FedGraph-VASP [46] (code "upon acceptance"; Elliptic Bitcoin and an Ethereum data set, not AMLworld) and FC2L [47] (no code or results found).

**Differentially private federated training.** The standard private baseline is federated averaging with differentially private gradient steps over the 30 bank views as clients, built with Flower and Opacus (Apache-2.0 libraries, `docs/t1_week0.md`). [CHECK: add references for federated averaging, DP-SGD, Flower and Opacus; none of them is in the proposal or in plan.md section 18] Its privacy guarantee is stated in a different unit from ours. A client-level guarantee protects a bank's whole dataset, an edge-level guarantee protects one transaction as ours does, and training-time ε is not the same quantity as the per-question ε we spend, so the comparison (H10) is plotted on common axes with explicit unit labels and a caveat paragraph. Differential privacy itself is as defined by Dwork and Roth [32].

**Private set counts.** Purcell et al. [36] give a differentially private protocol for estimating the size of the intersection of two parties' sets, so that two parties learn only how many items their lists share. We use this idea to let a bank ask "how many accounts in this set, defined relative to the alert, also appear in that set at another bank?" Plain hashing is not private, because a bank could hash every possible account identifier and match them back. We simulate the protocol's noisy output and do not build the cryptography.

**Hackathon demonstrations.** Two submissions to a lablab.ai hackathon [22] are described in the proposal: one applies federated analytics to cross-bank AML and AEGIS is an autonomous financial-crime investigation system; the proposal says that agents asking other banks questions under a privacy budget appear there without data, baselines or evaluation. AEGIS is built from 15 agents on CrewAI and LangGraph, and its code carries no licence (`docs/external_code_check.md`).

**What is left open.** Federated and pooled approaches are all-or-nothing: banks train jointly or compute on pooled data, and they are not used for per-alert questions. Private set counts have not been applied to following a trail of money across banks. The demonstrations in [22] ask questions under a budget but have no data, baselines or measurement. This work measures the privacy-versus-detection curve for per-alert questions, with set counts, against sharing raw records and against private federated training (RQ1, RQ6; H1, H2, H10).

## 2.3 LLM agents for fraud and AML

**Investigation agents.** Sharma [3] combines graph features, model explanations and an agentic case investigation, and reports the result that motivates part of this work: the LLM investigation agent made worse calls than the simple model it was meant to help, because it trusted its own convincing-sounding reasoning. The author's code is public under the MIT licence (`docs/external_code_check.md`), so we run a faithful port with our backbone in place of the original Groq-hosted model. Torres et al. [6] triage AML alerts with an LLM using subgraph evidence, citations and counterfactual checks. The paper specifies an evidence bundle with per-type quotas, a structured output with cited evidence, a verifier that rejects non-existent citations or inconsistent amounts and dates and triggers a repair step, and decision and evidence counterfactuals under an edit budget; it does not name a backbone or release prompts or code, so our version is a labelled reimplementation, and its published numbers (on SynthAML10, AMLSim and Elliptic, not AMLworld) cannot be checked on our data (`docs/external_code_check.md`). Co-Investigator AI [45] is a multi-agent system that produces suspicious-activity-report narratives; it releases no prompts, model, thresholds or decision, so the mapping from its output to a probability is ours and labelled. FinFRE-RAG [28] retrieves similar labelled fraud cases for an LLM on structured financial data. Palma [4] lets a calibrated formula, not the agent, make the final call, which is the design we compare in H7. FAA [5] is an LLM-based framework for credit card fraud investigations, and AEGIS [22] is the hackathon system above.

**Evidence about LLMs in fraud work.** Operational Evidence Gaps for LLMs in Fraud Detection and Trust-and-Safety Workflows [15] observes that fraud-LLM papers rarely report what the LLM adds, whether its evidence can be trusted, and what each decision costs. [CHECK: the repo states this point only through the proposal's one-line summary of [15]; confirm the paper's own wording and scope before relying on it]

**What is left open.** These agents investigate with one institution's full view, and their evidence is not tested before it is used. None asks other banks questions. This work runs Torres et al. [6] and Sharma [3] in two modes, native (as published, one bank's view) and ported (given our question tool with the same ε), and scores every LLM system twice, end to end and questions-only, so that asking better questions can be separated from making better final calls (RQ6, H9). Back-testing of every question and a formula in place of LLM text are our answers to the failure in [3] (RQ2, H4, H7).

## 2.4 General agent frameworks and information-seeking agents

**Tool-use and planning loops.** ReAct [49], Reflexion [50] and ReWOO [51] (with plan-and-execute) are the general tool-use loops that serve as the core external comparisons; LATS [52], LLMCompiler [53] and multi-agent debate [55] with AutoGen [56] add search, parallel tool calls (which bear on RQ5) and multiple cooperating agents. DSPy [61] is used to tune a ReAct agent with MIPROv2 on validation alerts with the Brier score as the objective; this is the strongest tuned external agent, so the comparison is not against a straw man. CodeAct [54] is a stretch comparison. Official code exists for most of these under permissive licences; the loops are short and the tools differ, so we reimplement the control loop in our harness with the published prompts and algorithms (the debate and Uncertainty of Thoughts repositories carry no licence and are reimplemented from the papers) (`docs/external_code_check.md`).

**Choosing what to ask.** Uncertainty of Thoughts [57], BED-LLM [10], MediQ [58], GraphDx [11] and LLM-elicited active feature acquisition [12] choose what information to request next; BED-LLM does so by an expected-information-gain procedure with Bayesian experimental design, GraphDx is a cost-aware multi-agent framework for sequential diagnosis, and NOL* [13] uses reinforcement learning to explore partially observed networks. The proposal's Table 1 records that classical planners often match LLMs at choosing what to ask. Our non-LLM planners (random, fixed order, expected information gain, a learned policy, and the enumerator of every expressible question) are the test of that claim on money trails across banks (H3). An optional reinforcement-learning planner [27], [41] is in the stretch tier.

**Memory frameworks.** ExpeL [59] and Agent Workflow Memory [60] are compared with our strategy library in the stretch tier, as library replacements (plan section 7.1).

**What is left open.** These methods are not tested on money trails across banks, where each question costs privacy budget, answers are noisy, and the other banks reply with latency. We port them into one harness that routes every question through the same gate and privacy accountant, with the same backbone and caps, so that only the control loop differs (RQ2, RQ6; H3, H9).

## 2.5 Error-controlled use of LLM ideas

**Keeping an idea only if it passes a test.** CAAFE [23] lets an LLM propose features and POPPER [33] has an agent falsify hypotheses; the proposal groups them as keeping an LLM's ideas only if they pass a statistical test. POPPER's e-values remain valid when data are added and the experiment is stopped at any time, which is the property we need when an LLM proposes thousands of candidate questions. We follow it, with the e-value false-discovery-rate procedure of Wang and Ramdas [37] over each batch of candidate questions, and an online e-value rule (e-LOND, Xu and Ramdas [68]) for new questions that arrive at test time.

**Conformal prediction and cascades.** Our alert intake uses class-conditional conformal prediction so that an alert reaches the system only when the bank's model cannot confidently rule out laundering; selective conformal risk control [38] bounds the misses among the transactions the model clears on its own, and time weighting for non-exchangeable data [39] handles day-to-day drift. Fast decisions use the System One models of the proposal: a commercial model that we mention but do not use [29], SemIf [30] (training-free typed decisions from a frozen language model's answer probabilities, MIT) and Laya [31] (a small non-autoregressive typed decision model with calibrated outputs, Apache-2.0), next to our own fine-tuned 4B model, with Conformal Cascade [35] deciding when to hand a case to the larger model (the error bound is 2α with two tiers; the tighter α needs an extra condition that we check on validation). RouteLLM [62] and FrugalGPT [63] are the cascade-style comparisons for the fast model. The three-way decision (auto-clear, human review, auto-escalate) follows FCAC [40], whose error control relies on randomly audited past cases and an assumption about how fast risk changes; we simulate audits and call the result "controlled under FCAC's assumption", not guaranteed.

**What is left open.** CAAFE and POPPER were applied to offline features and science hypotheses, and the conformal and cascade methods were not tested inside a multi-agent system with delayed labels and drift. We apply error control to LLM-invented questions in a live, budgeted, multi-bank setting and report the achieved error rates next to the targets (RQ2, RQ3; H4, H5).

## 2.6 Retrieval and memory

**Selecting graph evidence.** SubgraphRAG [25] and G-Retriever [26] pick the useful, connected part of a graph for an LLM. Our evidence retriever combines a small edge scorer in the style of SubgraphRAG, trained to predict whether a transaction belongs to a laundering pattern, with a prize-collecting Steiner tree step in the style of G-Retriever that joins the top edges into one connected, time-respecting trail of 40 edges. Evidence selection is compared with GNNExplainer [64], PGExplainer [65] and personalised PageRank (plan section 7.3).

**Reusing what worked.** ReasoningBank [34] keeps short strategies learned from both successes and failures; LivingRAG [24] augments graph RAG with experience; FinFRE-RAG [28] retrieves similar fraud cases. Our strategy library follows the ReasoningBank idea, with failure notes coming only from back-test statistics over many alerts and the library frozen before testing.

**What is left open.** SubgraphRAG and G-Retriever work on text knowledge graphs, not transaction graphs, and the memory methods were developed for text or web tasks, where reusing past cases by account would be harmless. Here, filing by account would leak, because the same laundering rings reappear later in AMLworld. We file strategies by the shape of the situation and run the by-account version as an ablation to show the difference (contribution 3); the retriever's effect is tested in RQ4 (H6).

## 2.7 Prompt-injection defences

CaMeL [14] defeats prompt injections by design, by treating untrusted content as data and not as instructions; we adopt the same principle for the one short text note that a partner bank's reply may carry, as real 314(b) replies can. The note never enters the decision, but the planner reads it, so it is where hidden instructions are planted. Spotlighting [66] and StruQ [67] are the other defences we compare with. Our injection set has four attack types of 100 each (direct instruction, fake authority, tool-call smuggling and data-exfiltration request), and we report how often injected text gets an agent to propose a bad action with and without our rules checker, for our system and for every external agent. **What is left open.** The proposal and plan do not claim any existing defence is evaluated on notes in other banks' replies inside an AML investigation loop; we add the test set and the with-and-without-checker measurement (section 8).

## 2.8 Regulation

Two regimes let banks ask each other about specific accounts. In the United States, 31 CFR 1010.540 [16] covers voluntary information sharing among financial institutions and FinCEN's Section 314(b) fact sheet [17] describes it; the repo's summary of the gate rule is that an institution that has notified FinCEN may share information with another participating institution to identify and report possible money laundering, and must keep procedures to protect the security and confidentiality of what it receives (`configs/compliance/rules.yaml`, which states that these summaries are not legal advice). [CHECK: verify the summary of 31 CFR 1010.540 against the regulation text before it is quoted in the report] In Singapore, the Monetary Authority of Singapore's Notice FSM-N02 and Guidelines on COSMIC [18] govern requests between participants. The two regimes differ, which is why the compliance gate must find which legal basis applies to a question. Red flags come from the FFIEC BSA/AML Examination Manual, Appendix F [76], and the Guidelines to MAS Notice 626 (Banks), Appendix B [77]; our v0 corpus contains 44 red flags, 28 from the FFIEC manual and 16 from the MAS guidelines (`configs/compliance/red_flags.yaml`). The FATF red-flag indicator reports [19] are planned but are not yet in the corpus because the PDFs could not be fetched (HTTP 403). The FFIEC text states that the mere presence of a red flag is not by itself evidence of criminal activity, and the MAS guidelines say a combination of situations may indicate a suspicious transaction; we treat red flags as hints for which questions to write, not as labels.

**What is left open.** The regulations define who may ask what; they do not say how much a small, noisy answer is worth, and they do not govern an LLM agent's actions. The compliance gate allows only question and action types with a legal basis and blocks everything else (section 5), and the privacy accountant turns the information a question can reveal into a budget that is counted (section 8).

## 2.9 Summary of the gap

Cross-bank work shares everything or nothing; LLM investigators assume they can see everything; and nobody tests an LLM's evidence before using it. The closest work in each group is above, and each group's open point maps to a research question: the privacy-versus-detection curve for questions (RQ1), the error-controlled LLM planner (RQ2), the head-to-head comparisons (RQ6), the fast model (RQ3) and the evidence retriever (RQ4).
